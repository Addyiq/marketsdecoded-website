Portfolio Cyber Hygiene for Operating Partners

The basics to expect, the questions to ask and the governance routine that keeps cyber exposure visible across the portfolio.

By Markets Decoded · Risk practice

Cyber risk has become a value creation issue. A serious incident at a portfolio company can halt operations, damage customer relationships, trigger regulatory obligations and complicate an exit. Yet many sponsors still treat cyber as an IT matter that belongs to each company’s management.

That leaves operating partners with a blind spot. They are accountable for value across the portfolio, but they often lack a consistent view of how exposed each company is, or whether the basics are in place.

This playbook sets out a practical approach: the hygiene basics to expect, the questions to ask, and a routine that keeps cyber on the agenda without turning operating partners into security specialists.

Why the portfolio view matters

Portfolio companies vary widely in size, maturity and resources. A larger platform may have a dedicated security leader. A recent add-on may rely on one IT generalist or an outsourced provider. Integration projects, new systems and acquired networks all create new exposure, often faster than anyone tracks it.

From the sponsor’s perspective, the risk is not only in each company. It is in the inconsistency. Without a common standard, the operating team cannot compare exposure, prioritize investment or give the investment committee and lenders a credible answer when they ask.

Start from the outside in

Attackers usually begin by looking at what a company exposes to the internet: domains, servers, remote access services, email configuration and cloud assets. An outside-in review shows the same picture without needing access to internal systems or lengthy questionnaires.

It is also a fair starting point across a portfolio. It applies the same lens to every company, whatever its internal maturity, and gives the operating team a consistent baseline.

The hygiene basics

Every portfolio company, whatever its size, should be able to show that a core set of controls is in place:

  1. An accurate asset inventory. You cannot protect what you do not know you have.
  2. Multi-factor authentication on email, remote access and privileged accounts.
  3. Timely patching of internet-facing systems and critical software.
  4. Tested backups, stored separately from production, with a recovery time the business can accept.
  5. Endpoint protection and monitoring on laptops and servers.
  6. Access management that removes access promptly when people leave or change roles, and limits administrator rights.
  7. Email security that protects against spoofing and phishing.
  8. An incident response plan that names who does what, including legal, communications and the sponsor, and that has been rehearsed.
  9. Third-party oversight of vendors and service providers with access to systems or data.
  10. Security awareness training for staff, refreshed regularly.

None of these is advanced. All of them are frequently incomplete.

The questions to ask

At each portfolio company, an operating partner should be able to get clear answers to these questions:

  • Who is accountable for cyber risk, and how do they report to the CEO and the board?
  • What does the company expose to the internet, and when was that last reviewed?
  • Which of the hygiene basics are fully in place, partly in place or missing?
  • When was the incident response plan last tested, and what did the test reveal?
  • How quickly are critical vulnerabilities fixed once they are found?
  • Which third parties could cause an incident, and how are they monitored?
  • Do the controls in place match what the cyber insurance policy requires?

If the answers are vague or inconsistent, that is the finding.

Build a governance routine

Hygiene is not a one-time exercise. Exposure changes every time a company adds a system, a vendor or an acquisition. The goal is a routine that keeps the view current.

WhenActivity
At acquisitionOutside-in exposure review during diligence or the first 100 days.
OngoingContinuous monitoring of external exposure across the portfolio.
QuarterlyA short cyber section in each board pack: exposure trend, open critical items and incidents.
AnnuallyAn incident response exercise and a review of the hygiene basics.
Before exitRemediate known issues so cyber does not become a buyer’s diligence finding.

The point is consistency. A common format across companies shows the operating team which businesses need attention and whether remediation is actually happening.

Governance matters as much as technology

When we advised a sovereign wealth fund on cyber resilience, the work combined strategy, governance and technology. Leadership enacted corrective governance measures, and a turn-key SaaS platform was put in place to reduce external cyber risk and help mitigate threats. The lesson for sponsors is that tools alone do not close the gap. Clear accountability and a regular review routine are what make the tools effective.
