Sales Comp Redesign in 8 Weeks

A practical, week-by-week sequence for redesigning sales incentives without disrupting the quarter.

By Markets Decoded · Go-To-Market practice

Most leadership teams agree their sales compensation plan could be better. Fewer act on it, because redesign feels risky. Get it wrong and you lose good sellers, confuse customers or overrun the incentive budget. Get the timing wrong and you disrupt a quarter the sponsor is counting on.

The answer is not to avoid the change. It is to run it as a disciplined, time-boxed project with clear decisions at each step. Eight weeks is enough to move from diagnosis to a plan that is ready to launch at the start of the next period, provided the team stays focused.

Here is the sequence we use.

Before you start

Three things need to be in place:

  1. An owner and a decision-maker. Usually the CEO, with the CRO and CFO as co-owners. The operating partner should agree the objectives up front.
  2. Clean data. Bookings, revenue, margin and payout history by seller for at least the most recent plan period, plus territory and account assignments.
  3. A launch date. Ideally the start of a new fiscal year or quarter. Working back from it keeps the project honest.

Weeks 1–2: Diagnose

The goal is to understand what the current plan actually rewards, as opposed to what it was meant to reward.

  • Interview the CEO, CRO, CFO, frontline sales managers and a cross-section of sellers.
  • Map every component of the current plan: base, variable, accelerators, spiffs, caps, clawbacks and exceptions.
  • Compare payout with performance. Who earned what, for what, and did it line up with the outcomes the business needed?
  • Review quota setting: how quotas were set, how attainment was distributed and whether territories were balanced.

The output is a short diagnostic: what is working, what is out of line with the value creation plan, and what the redesign needs to solve.

Weeks 3–4: Design

Start from the drivers, not from the existing plan.

  • Agree the few outcomes the plan should reward, tied to the sponsor’s thesis: for example, new customer revenue, priority products, margin or retention.
  • Set the mix between base and variable pay for each role.
  • Choose the measures and weightings, keeping the number of components small.
  • Design accelerators and thresholds that reward performance above target without encouraging sandbagging.
  • Define a quota method based on territory or account potential.

Build two or three design options, not one. Leadership makes better choices when it can compare trade-offs.

Weeks 5–6: Model and test

This is where most redesigns either earn credibility or lose it.

  • Model each option against historical performance: what would each seller have earned under the new plan?
  • Run scenarios for the coming period: below target, on target and above target.
  • Confirm that total cost of sales stays within the agreed envelope in each scenario.
  • Identify the individual winners and losers, and decide how to handle material changes for specific sellers, such as a transition provision.
  • Pressure-test the plan with a few trusted sales managers. If they cannot explain it, sellers will not understand it either.

Finance should sign off on the model before anything is communicated.

Weeks 7–8: Prepare to launch

A good plan with a poor launch will underperform.

  • Finalize plan documents, with clear definitions, payout timing and a dispute process.
  • Prepare individual plan letters showing each seller their quota and how they will be paid.
  • Train frontline managers first, so they can answer questions with confidence.
  • Hold a launch session led by the CEO and CRO that explains what is changing and how it links to the company’s strategy.
  • Set up reporting so sellers can track their own attainment and earnings during the period.

The eight weeks at a glance

WeeksFocusKey decision
1–2DiagnoseWhat does the current plan really reward?
3–4DesignWhich outcomes and measures should the new plan pay for?
5–6Model and testWhich option, at what cost, with what transition?
7–8Prepare to launchIs every manager ready to explain it?

After launch: the first period

The work does not end at launch. Review results monthly through the first period: payouts against forecast, changes in deal mix and discounting, and feedback from sellers. Agree in advance what would trigger an adjustment, and resist one-off exceptions that undermine the design.

Why this works in a sponsor context

Sponsor-backed companies need revenue growth that lasts through the hold period and shows up in the equity story. A comp redesign that is tied to the value creation plan, modeled before launch and communicated well delivers that without adding fixed cost.

We saw this with a mid-market healthcare diagnostics company, where a detailed, drivers-based sales compensation model, aligned with a wider transformation, helped mobilize the sales function and contributed to a 15% revenue lift.
