The Sponsor-Backed Cash Conversion Benchmark

Cash conversion is where the investment thesis meets the bank balance. Why it matters under sponsor ownership, what our forthcoming benchmark will measure, and how operating partners can use it.

By Markets Decoded · Financial practice

Every value creation plan has an EBITDA line. Fewer have a cash line that the board reviews with the same discipline. Yet in a leveraged capital structure, cash is what services debt, funds add-ons and, at exit, supports the story a buyer is asked to underwrite.

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) is the simplest single measure of how hard a business makes its cash work. We are building the Sponsor-Backed Cash Conversion Benchmark to give operating partners, deal teams and portfolio CFOs a credible reference point for it. This article explains why it matters, what the benchmark will measure, how we are building it and how to use it once it is published.

Why cash conversion matters more under sponsor ownership

The formula is familiar: CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO. Days sales outstanding measures how long customers take to pay. Days inventory outstanding measures how long stock sits before it is sold. Days payables outstanding measures how long the company takes to pay its suppliers. The result is the number of days the business funds its own operating cycle.

For a sponsor-backed company, each of those days has a price. Cash tied up in receivables and inventory is cash that is not paying down debt or funding the next acquisition. When covenants tighten, it can be the difference between a constructive lender conversation and a difficult one. And because working capital usually grows with revenue, a growth plan built on a long cycle can consume cash faster than it creates EBITDA.

Cash conversion is also one of the few levers management can move within a quarter or two, without new capital, new hires or new systems. In our own work, an energy parts distributor improved its cash conversion cycle by 22% once the board and leadership had a reliable 13-week view of cash. A direct mail and marketing company in a turnaround improved its cycle by 14% while managing liquidity against its covenants. Both results came from operating discipline, not financial engineering.

The problem with “good” and “bad”

Ask a portfolio CFO whether the company’s CCC is good and the honest answer is usually “compared with what?”

Internal history tells you about the trend, not about the potential. Public company filings describe large, diversified businesses whose customer and supplier terms are often quite different from a mid-market company’s. Rules of thumb ignore sector, size and business model altogether.

As a result, working capital targets in many value creation plans are set by negotiation rather than evidence. Some are too soft to matter. Others are aggressive enough to strain supplier relationships or service levels. A relevant benchmark gives the sponsor and management a shared fact base for that conversation.

What the benchmark measures

The benchmark will report the cash conversion cycle and each of its components:

  1. Days sales outstanding (DSO): how quickly revenue turns into cash.
  2. Days inventory outstanding (DIO): how long inventory is held, where inventory is material to the business model.
  3. Days payables outstanding (DPO): how the company uses supplier terms.
  4. Cash conversion cycle (CCC): the net result.

Each measure will be cut by sector and by size band, so a mid-market distributor is compared with distributors of a similar scale, not with software companies or global conglomerates. Where a sample is too thin to be meaningful, we will say so rather than publish a figure.

How we are building it

The benchmark combines two sources, shown side by side rather than blended.

Anonymized engagement data. Our Financial practice baselines working capital at the start of every cash engagement. With client permission and full anonymization, those baselines form the sponsor-backed side of the dataset. No company, sponsor or lender will be identifiable, and we will not publish a figure for any group small enough to point to a single business.

Public-company comparables. For each sector, we will draw on the reported financials of listed peers to provide a broader reference point. Public companies are imperfect comparators for the middle market, which is exactly why we keep the two sources separate.

Each edition will set out our definitions and method, including how we treat seasonality, differences in revenue recognition and one-off items, so readers can judge the numbers for themselves.

How operating partners should read it

A benchmark is the start of a question, not the answer. We suggest using it in four ways:

  1. Locate the gap by component. A CCC in line with peers can hide weak collections offset by stretched payables. The components tell you where to look.
  2. Test the plan’s working capital assumptions. If a value creation plan assumes a cycle well outside the sector range, it needs either a specific explanation or a revision.
  3. Prioritize across the portfolio. Comparing each company’s components with its own sector and size band shows where an operating partner’s time is likely to release the most cash.
  4. Frame the conversation with management. An external reference point moves the discussion from opinion to evidence, which usually makes it shorter and more productive.

Two cautions. First, business model matters. A company with project billing or consignment inventory will look different from its peers for structural reasons, and that is not a problem to fix. Second, the goal is not to hit the median. It is to understand why you differ and decide whether the difference is costing you cash.

What to do before it is published

You do not need the benchmark to start. Ask each portfolio CFO for the current CCC and its three components, calculated on a consistent basis, with the trend over recent quarters. If that request takes more than a few days to answer, you have learned something important about cash visibility already.
