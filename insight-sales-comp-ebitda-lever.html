Sales comp is your cheapest EBITDA lever

Before you add headcount, tools or new markets, look at what your incentive plan is actually paying for. Redesigning it often costs less and moves faster.

By Markets Decoded · Go-To-Market practice

When a sponsor-backed company needs more revenue, the usual answers are expensive. Hire more sellers. Buy a new sales tool. Enter a new market. Launch a new product. Each has its place, and each adds fixed cost and execution risk before it adds revenue.

There is a lever that sits in almost every business and is often overlooked: the sales compensation plan. It already exists. It is already paid. And every day, it shapes what the sales team chooses to do with its time.

Your comp plan is already a strategy

Sellers are rational. They read the compensation plan closely and work out how to maximize their payout. If the plan pays on bookings regardless of margin, they will discount to close. If it pays the same for renewals as for new logos, they will farm existing accounts. If quotas are set top-down without regard to territory potential, the strongest sellers hit target early and coast, while others give up.

In other words, the comp plan is a strategy document, whether or not anyone designed it as one. The question is whether it matches the strategy in the value creation plan.

Often it does not. Plans accumulate over years: a spiff added here, an exception there, an accelerator for a product that is no longer a priority. Nobody owns the whole design, so nobody notices when it starts paying for the wrong things.

Why it is cheap

Redesigning sales compensation usually does not need new budget. The total incentive pool can stay the same; what changes is what it rewards. That makes it different from most growth levers.

LeverNew fixed costTime to effect
Add sales headcountYesLong: hiring and ramp-up
New sales technologyYesMedium: implementation and adoption
New market or productYesLong: build and launch
Comp plan redesignUsually noneShort: the next plan period

This is not a ranking of value. Some companies genuinely need more sellers or better tools. But comp redesign is usually the cheapest place to start, and getting it right makes every other commercial investment work harder.

What a drivers-based plan looks like

The most effective redesigns start from drivers, not from last year’s plan.

  1. Start with the value creation plan. Which revenue matters most to the sponsor’s thesis: new customers, specific products, specific segments, margin or retention?
  2. Map the behaviors that produce it. What do sellers need to do more of, or less of, to deliver that revenue?
  3. Pay for those behaviors. Weight the plan toward the measures that matter, with a small number of components that sellers can understand and track.
  4. Set quotas from territory potential. Fair, achievable quotas keep the whole team engaged, not just the top performers.
  5. Model the cost before launch. Run the new plan against historical performance and realistic scenarios, so finance knows what it will pay at different outcomes.
  6. Communicate clearly. A plan that sellers do not understand will not change behavior.

For a mid-market healthcare diagnostics company in the middle of a broader transformation, we built a detailed, drivers-based sales compensation model aligned with the transformation’s goals. It helped mobilize the sales function behind the plan and contributed to a 15% lift in revenue.

Common pitfalls

  • Too many components. If a seller needs a spreadsheet to work out what a deal is worth, the plan is too complex.
  • Caps that punish success. Hard caps can push top performers to delay deals into the next period.
  • Paying on revenue the company does not collect. Where practical, tie payout to collected or recognized revenue, so comp supports cash as well as growth.
  • Changing the plan mid-period without warning. Trust in the plan is part of what makes it work.
  • Ignoring sales managers. Frontline managers need incentives aligned with their team’s results.

Questions to ask this quarter

For operating partners and portfolio CEOs, a few questions show quickly whether the comp plan is helping or hurting:

  • Can the CRO explain in one sentence what the plan is designed to reward?
  • Does it reward the revenue the value creation plan depends on?
  • Who hit quota last year, and was that down to performance or to how quotas were set?
  • How much discounting happens in the last weeks of each period?
  • When did someone last model the plan’s total cost against different outcomes?

If the answers are vague, the comp plan is probably leaving revenue on the table, and fixing it is likely to be the fastest commercial win available.
