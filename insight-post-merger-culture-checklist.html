Post-Merger Culture Integration Checklist

What to measure, decide and communicate in the first 90 days after an add-on closes.

By Markets Decoded · Cultural practice

Add-on acquisitions sit at the heart of many buy-and-build strategies. The financial integration usually gets a detailed plan: systems, reporting, synergies. The people side often gets a town hall and a new org chart.

That imbalance is costly. Synergy cases assume teams will work together. When they do not, the costs arrive slowly: key people leave, customers notice service gaps, decisions take longer and the integration timeline slips.

Culture integration does not have to be vague. It can be run with the same discipline as any other workstream. This checklist covers what to measure, decide and communicate in the first 90 days after close.

How to use this checklist

Treat it as a working document for the integration steering committee, not a one-off exercise for HR. Give each item an owner and a date, and review it at the same meetings where you track systems and synergies. Some items will not apply to every deal. A small tuck-in that keeps its own brand and leadership needs a lighter touch than a full combination of teams. The sequence still holds, though: prepare, measure, decide, embed, and communicate throughout.

Before close: prepare

  • Identify the people critical to the value of the deal, whether for customer relationships, technical knowledge or leadership, and plan how you will keep them.
  • Compare how each organization makes decisions, sets priorities and communicates. Note the obvious differences early.
  • Agree who leads the integration on each side and how much of their time it needs.
  • Draft the day-one message: why the deal happened, what changes, what does not, and when people will hear more.

Days 1–30: Measure

The first month is about listening and building a fact base.

  • Run a short culture assessment across both organizations, using surveys, interviews and focus groups at several levels.
  • Map where the two cultures differ in ways that matter to the integration plan: decision rights, pace, appetite for risk, customer approach and accountability.
  • Find friction points early, especially where teams must now share processes, customers or systems.
  • Confirm the critical-people list and check in with each person directly.
  • Baseline the measures you will track, such as retention of key talent, engagement and integration milestones.

Days 31–60: Decide

Uncertainty is the biggest driver of disengagement after a deal. The second month is about making the decisions people are waiting for.

  • Confirm the leadership structure and reporting lines, and communicate them.
  • Decide which processes, systems and policies will be standardized, and on what timeline.
  • Define decision rights for the combined organization: who decides what, and who must be consulted.
  • Choose the few behaviors the combined organization will keep, and the ones it will leave behind. Be specific.
  • Assign each friction point from the assessment to a named owner with a deadline.

Days 61–90: Embed

The third month is about making the new way of working stick.

  • Launch leadership routines for the combined organization: regular leadership meetings, shared performance reviews and joint planning.
  • Align incentives so people are rewarded for the combined organization’s results, not only their legacy unit’s.
  • Set up cross-team working groups on real business problems rather than team-building exercises.
  • Re-run a pulse survey and compare the results with the baseline.
  • Report integration progress to the board alongside financial synergies.

Throughout: communicate

  • Communicate early, often and consistently. Silence gets filled with rumor.
  • Make sure leaders from both organizations deliver the same messages.
  • Provide safe channels for questions and concerns, and respond visibly.
  • Acknowledge what is being lost, not only what is being gained.

Warning signs to watch for

  1. Key people from the acquired company leaving or disengaging.
  2. Teams still talking about “us” and “them” well after close.
  3. Decisions escalating to the top because nobody is sure who owns them.
  4. Duplicate processes running in parallel with no plan to merge them.
  5. Information not flowing between legacy teams.

These are the same patterns we identified in a culture assessment for the country office of a global non-profit during an organization-wide merger of departments: frictions, communication gaps, structural roadblocks and silos. Naming them clearly, and giving leadership a roadmap to address them, contributed to a 20% gain in efficiency.

A note for operating partners

Culture integration should not be left entirely to HR. Put it on the integration steering committee’s agenda at every meeting, with measures as concrete as the financial synergies. At each review, ask to see the assessment results, the retention status of critical people and the friction log.
