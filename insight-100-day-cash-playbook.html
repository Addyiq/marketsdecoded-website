The 100-Day Cash Playbook

Cash is the first proof that a new owner’s plan is working. A practical sequence for installing a 13-week cash rhythm and releasing working capital inside the first 100 days.

By Markets Decoded · Financial practice

The first 100 days after a platform close set the tone for the hold. Management is learning what the sponsor expects, lenders are watching the first compliance certificates, and the deal team is testing whether the thesis survives contact with the business.

Cash is the most honest early signal. EBITDA initiatives take time to show up in the numbers. A well-run cash program can show results inside the first quarter of ownership, and it builds the reporting discipline that everything else depends on.

This playbook sets out the sequence we use. It is deliberately simple. The hard part is not the method. It is doing it every week.

Days 1–15: Build the 13-week view

A 13-week cash flow forecast is a rolling, week-by-week view of receipts and disbursements, built bottom-up from customers, suppliers, payroll, debt service and capital spending. Unlike a monthly P&L forecast, it shows timing: when cash actually arrives and when it leaves.

Start with what the business can produce now, not with a perfect model. The first version should:

  1. Start from the actual opening bank balance and available facility headroom.
  2. Forecast receipts from the open receivables ledger and expected collections, not from revenue.
  3. Forecast disbursements from the payables ledger, payroll calendar, rent, taxes and debt service.
  4. Flag the weeks where liquidity is tightest.
  5. Name one owner for each line.

Then roll it forward every week and compare forecast with actual. The variance review is where the learning happens. Within a few cycles, the team knows which assumptions are reliable and which are hopeful.

When we built a 13-week model for a mid-market energy parts distributor, the immediate value was visibility. The board and leadership could finally see the company’s cash position and flows clearly. That visibility became the foundation for a 22% improvement in the cash conversion cycle, and it helped keep the company out of distress.

Days 15–30: Baseline working capital

Next, establish where cash is tied up. The cash conversion cycle (CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO) gives the headline, but the work happens one level down.

  • Receivables: aging by customer, disputed invoices, billing delays, credit terms that differ from contract, and collections effort.
  • Inventory: slow-moving and obsolete stock, safety stock settings, purchasing rules, and items held for customers who have not committed.
  • Payables: actual payment timing against agreed terms, early payments, and supplier terms that have drifted.

The output is a short list of specific, owned opportunities, each with an estimate of the cash it releases and the weeks in which it lands. Resist the long list. A handful of well-owned actions beats a long list of orphaned ones.

Days 30–75: Work the three levers in parallel

Receivables, inventory and payables usually sit with different owners: finance, operations and procurement. Working them in sequence wastes weeks. Work them in parallel, with one weekly meeting where each owner reports progress against the 13-week forecast.

LeverPractical actions
ReceivablesInvoice on time and accurately, resolve disputes quickly, align credit terms with contracts, and prioritize collections by value and age.
InventoryStop reordering slow movers, rebalance safety stock, clear obsolete items and tighten purchasing approvals.
PayablesPay to agreed terms rather than early, consolidate payment runs and renegotiate terms where the relationship allows.

Two guardrails matter. Do not fix payables by damaging critical suppliers. A stretched supplier who stops shipping costs more than the cash released. And do not cut inventory below what service levels need, because lost orders show up in EBITDA.

Days 75–100: Lock in the rhythm

By now the business should have a forecast it trusts and a set of working capital actions in flight. The final phase makes the discipline permanent.

  1. Embed the 13-week forecast in the weekly management meeting and the monthly board pack.
  2. Set targets for the CCC and each component for the hold period, owned by named executives.
  3. Link working capital to incentives where it makes sense, so cash discipline outlasts the program.
  4. Document the process so it survives staff turnover.

Extend it to covenants when it matters

In a turnaround or a tight capital structure, the same disciplines extend to the lender relationship. For a direct mail and marketing company, we paired liquidity management with a model showing how financial performance would affect covenants. That gave leadership and the sponsor a clear view of headroom, and the company improved its cash conversion cycle by 14%.

What operating partners should watch

Three signs the program is working: forecast accuracy improves week by week, the owners of each lever report without being chased, and the board conversation shifts from “where is the cash?” to “what do we do with it?”

Three signs it is not: the forecast is rebuilt from scratch every month, actions have no named owner, and variances are explained but never acted on.
